Section 1 · Introduction

What is Microbiology?

Microbiology is the science that studies living organisms that cannot be seen with the naked eye — known as microorganisms or microbes.

Microorganisms are commonly known as germs. They are present in the air, on surfaces, in water, on our skin, and inside our bodies — which is exactly why a sterile manufacturing environment must be actively controlled.

  • Invisible to the naked eye — we rely on monitoring, not on sight
  • Present everywhere — personnel are the closest and most constant source
  • Some grow in conditions we would consider hostile

Micro = Small

Measured in micrometres. A single contaminated fingerprint can carry millions of organisms.

Bio = Life

They grow, multiply and adapt — provided the conditions favour them.

Logy = Study

Understanding them lets us predict, monitor and control them.

QC perspective: in 10 years of sterile QC, the single most common root cause of environmental excursions I have investigated is personnel behaviour — not equipment.

Section 1 · Introduction

Nature of Microorganisms

Pathogens

Microorganisms that cause disease. These are the ones we are most concerned about in a sterile facility.

Harmless

Most microorganisms are harmless to humans. Not every colony on a plate is a threat.

Beneficial

Some microorganisms found in the body are beneficial — for example, gut flora that help digest food.

The "wrong place" principle: when a microorganism enters a part of the body other than where it is intended to be, it can become harmful.

Example: E. coli normally lives in the colon, where it breaks down waste and produces vitamin K. If it enters the urinary tract, the same organism causes infection.

The same principle applies to product

A microorganism that is harmless on intact skin can be harmful once it enters a sterile injection, an open vial, or a patient's bloodstream. Route of entry defines risk.

Our control strategy

We do not try to kill everything everywhere. We prevent microorganisms from reaching the product in the first place — through gowning, aseptic technique, cleaning and monitoring.

Section 2 · Types of Microorganism

The 5 Major Types of Microorganisms

Bacteria

  • Single-celled organisms that multiply by binary fission
  • Found everywhere — air, water, skin, surfaces, dust
  • Typical cleanroom isolates: Staphylococcus, Micrococcus, Pseudomonas, Bacillus
  • Endospore formers (Bacillus, Clostridium) survive harsh conditions and resist many disinfectants

Why it matters in sterile manufacturing

Bacteria are the most frequently recovered organisms in environmental monitoring. Gram-positive cocci usually point to personnel; Gram-negative rods often point to water or moisture.

Fungi (Yeasts & Moulds)

  • Grow well in moist environments
  • Tolerate more acidic conditions than most bacteria
  • Moulds produce spores that travel easily in air currents
  • Common environmental isolates in cleanrooms and HVAC systems

Why it matters in sterile manufacturing

Fungal spores are resilient and can survive for long periods on surfaces. They are monitored using SDA (Sabouraud Dextrose Agar) alongside TSA.

Protozoa (Protists)

  • Single-celled organisms, generally larger than bacteria
  • Mostly water-borne
  • Some form cysts that are highly resistant to disinfection

Why it matters in sterile manufacturing

Rarely a direct product contaminant in aseptic processing, but relevant to water system control and to the broader microbiology picture.

Parasites (Rickettsia)

  • Rickettsia are obligate intracellular bacteria, usually transmitted by vectors such as ticks
  • Parasites generally require a host to complete their life cycle
  • Not cultivable on standard environmental monitoring media

Why it matters in sterile manufacturing

Not a typical cleanroom isolate, but part of the microbiology knowledge base — and relevant to personnel health and fitness-to-work assessments.

Viruses

  • The smallest of all microorganisms
  • Cannot reproduce on their own — they must use a host cell
  • They do not grow or multiply in product
  • They can be carried and transferred by people

Why it matters in sterile manufacturing

Control relies on aseptic behaviour, gowning and personal hygiene — not on product testing. A virus will not show up on a settle plate.

Microbial Growth Classification

Microorganisms are also classified by their oxygen requirement.

🫧 Aerobic Microbes

Live only in the presence of oxygen. If oxygen is excluded, they will not grow.

🚫 Anaerobic Microbes

Grow best in the absence of oxygen. Oxygen is toxic or inhibitory to them.

🔄 Facultative Anaerobic

Can grow in both the presence and the absence of oxygen. The most adaptable — and often the most difficult to control.

Practical implication: the same organism can behave differently in different parts of the process. A closed vessel, a stagnant water line and an open filling zone each favour a different population. This is why we monitor multiple locations — not just one.

Where we look for aerobes

Air, surfaces, gowning, settle plates, active air samples — the standard viable monitoring programme.

Where anaerobes hide

Purified water and WFI loops, stagnant lines, poorly drained equipment, biofilm in dead legs.

Section 3 · Contamination Control

Sources of Contamination

Contamination is any foreign environmental, chemical or biological matter. Select a source to see how it reaches the product.

PRODUCT

i) People — the number one source

  • Shedding of cells, skin flakes and oils
  • Inappropriate gowning
  • Exposed skin, hair or clothing
  • Incorrectly wearing cleanroom attire
  • Bringing additional items into clean areas
  • Jewellery, shoes, clothing, purses, keys, cell phones

ii) Equipment

  • Improper design — hard-to-clean surfaces, crevices, dead legs
  • Improper and ineffective cleaning — leading to cross contamination
  • Inadequate maintenance

iv) Materials / Containers / Closures

  • Contaminated incoming materials
  • Mishandled components
  • Improper storage
  • Cardboard, wood pallets and packing materials — never allowed in controlled areas

Water

  • Purified Water and Water for Injection are prime microbial habitats if not controlled
  • Biofilm formation in pipework, dead legs and stagnant loops
  • Monitored by viable counts, bioburden, filtration, pour plates and chemistry (pH, conductivity, TOC)

v) Environment — Air, Surfaces & Facilities

  • Air, surfaces, temperature, humidity and water
  • Insufficient cleaning
  • Inappropriate facility design and pest control issues
  • Cardboard, wood pallets and other particle generators

Section 3 · Contamination Control

Components of a Contamination Control Programme

Definition: proactive prevention of contamination — to ensure product is free of foreign matter, that appropriate control measures exist, that environments and product contact areas do not contribute to contamination, that gowning practices and materials/containers/closures are controlled, and that all of this is documented.

i) Compliance Assessment

Guidelines, regulations, process and facility evaluation.

ii) Product Testing

Chemical and microbiological purity using validated methods.

iii) Environmental Monitoring

Viable / non-viable, utilities, materials, components.

iv) Equipment Qualification & Cleaning Validation

Documented evidence of design, cleaning and maintenance.

v) Facility Design

Segregation, flow, sanitary design, pest control.

vi) Disinfectant Practices

Correct agent, correct contact time, correct surface.

vii) Training

Operators, lab personnel, engineers and support groups.

Remember: contamination control is a system. One weak component — usually training or behaviour — will compromise all the others.

Section 3 · Contamination Control

Environmental Monitoring — Viable Monitoring

Media

  • TSA — Tryptic Soy Agar (total aerobic microbial count)
  • SDA — Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (yeasts and moulds)

Air

  • Active air sampling — a known volume of air is drawn over the medium
  • Air filtration — HEPA filtration as the primary barrier
  • Passive air sampling — settle plates, exposed for a defined time

Surfaces

  • Contact plates — direct contact with flat surfaces
  • Swabs — for irregular, recessed or hard-to-reach areas

Static vs Dynamic

Static — monitoring with no activity in the area.
Dynamic — monitoring with activity, i.e. during operation. Dynamic results reflect the real manufacturing risk.

Physical Parameters

  • Temperature & Relative Humidity (R/H)
  • Differential pressures between classified areas

Controlled Access

A documented procedure must detail what can and cannot be brought into manufacturing areas.

Never permitted: cardboard, wood products/pallets, packing materials.

Environmental Monitoring — Water & Utilities

Microbiological Testing

  • Purified Water & WFI — sampled at defined points on a defined frequency
  • Viable counts — enumeration of recovered organisms
  • Bioburden — total microbial load of the sample
  • Filtration — membrane filtration through 0.45 µm membrane
  • Pour plates — for enumeration where appropriate

Chemical Testing

  • pH
  • Conductivity
  • TOC — Total Organic Carbon

Chemistry and microbiology go hand in hand: organic carbon feeds microorganisms, and conductivity excursions often precede microbial excursions.

Utility monitoring also covers gases, water and compressed air — any utility that contacts the product or a product-contact surface is a potential contamination route.
Operator reminder: never take a water sample from a flushed-and-forgotten point without following the sampling SOP. A bad sample is worse than no sample — it gives false assurance.

Evaluation of Incoming Goods

Contamination control starts before materials reach the cleanroom.

Raw Materials

Must meet the Certificate of Analysis (COA) and internal specifications.

Vendor Selection

Approved suppliers with a demonstrated quality history.

Quality

Conformance to specification — every batch, every time.

Bioburden

Microbial limits must be established and met.

Identity

Correct material — verified, not assumed.

Containers & Closures

Assessed for cleanliness and compatibility.

Certificate of Analysis

Reviewed against internal specification before release.

Cleanliness

Free from particulate and microbial contamination.

Storage & Handling

Controlled conditions, appropriate transfer and protection.

Transfer discipline: materials must be transferred into the cleanroom using the approved route and method. Outer packaging stays outside — always.

Section 3 · Contamination Control

Equipment Qualification & Facility Design

Equipment Qualification & Cleaning Validation

  • Documented studies to support the equipment
  • Equipment is of appropriate design
  • Materials of construction are suitable for the environment
  • Meets the intended need
  • Effective equipment cleaning & maintenance procedures
  • Cleaning history and maintenance history maintained
  • Compatibility of cleaning agents confirmed
  • Removal of cleaning agent confirmed

Facility Design & Control

  • Segregation of process areas from mechanical / utility areas
  • Orderly operational flows for personnel, materials and processes
  • Group similar types of operations
  • Sanitary design — cleanable, smooth, non-shedding surfaces
  • Pest control — baits and traps
  • Clean and orderly at all times
Compliance assessment underpins all of this — evaluated against NPRA and foreign agency requirements, and against the specific nature of the process (oral solid dose vs liquid, toxic compounds, dusty operations, open vs closed operations, single-use vs dedicated, room classification, barriers or isolators, and disinfectant selection).

Section 4 · Methods to Reduce Contamination

3 Common Methods to Reduce Microbial Contamination

Everything we have discussed so far comes down to three practical control methods.

1️⃣ Aseptic Technique

Procedures performed under sterile conditions — keeping an area completely free of microorganisms.

2️⃣ Disinfection

IPA & Sodium Hypochlorite — slow or prevent the growth of pathogens on surfaces and hands.

3️⃣ Sterilization

The process that renders an article, surface or medium free from all microorganisms, including spores.

Critical distinction: aseptic technique and disinfection alone are not effective against spores. Only sterilization kills spores.

Aseptic Technique

  • A procedure that is performed under sterile conditions
  • Includes medical and laboratory techniques dealing with cultures and human cells and tissue for transplantation
  • Keeping an area completely free of microorganisms
  • Only sterile objects can come in contact with sterile objects. Otherwise, they must be considered contaminated

Purpose in our environment

  • To prevent the access of microorganisms during sample preparation and testing
  • To prevent cross contamination from the environment and personnel to the sample we are working with
The one rule to remember: if a sterile object touches anything that is not sterile — or if you are unsure whether it is sterile — it is contaminated. Discard it and start again. There is no "probably still fine".

Tap to reveal
Antisepsis It is the process by which the growth of bacteria is inhibited — but they are not killed.
Know the difference: sterilization kills everything; disinfection kills pathogens; antisepsis only inhibits growth. Using the wrong one for the wrong application is a deviation waiting to happen.

Disinfection — IPA & Sodium Hypochlorite

  • Disinfection slows or prevents the growth of pathogens
  • Disinfecting a surface will "kill" the microscopic organisms as claimed on the label of that particular product
  • Effectiveness depends on correct concentration, correct contact time and correct application

Understand the environment before you disinfect

  • Cleanliness required
  • Type and amount of organic matter / microorganisms present
  • Surface type
  • Potential for deterioration or corrosion
  • Type of water available
  • Contact time

Isopropyl Alcohol (IPA)

Fast-acting, evaporates cleanly, ideal for glove sanitisation, small surfaces and transfer hatches. Not sporicidal — it does not kill bacterial spores.

Sodium Hypochlorite

Broader spectrum, with sporicidal activity at the correct concentration and contact time. Used for floors, walls and controlled surface disinfection. Corrosive to some materials — and must be rinsed where required.

The golden rule

Disinfectant applied and immediately wiped dry has not worked. Contact time is part of the method, not an optional extra.

Section 4 · Methods to Reduce Contamination

Sterilization

Sterilization uses validated methods to kill all microorganisms, including spores. Aseptic technique and disinfection alone are not effective against spores — sterilization is.

♨️ Autoclave

Uses steam under pressure to kill all microorganisms. The primary sterilization method in sterile manufacturing.

🧪 Chemical (Chemiclave)

Uses chemical solutions when heat cannot be used — for heat-sensitive instruments and materials.

🔥 Dry Heat

Used when heat or chemicals cannot be used. Requires higher temperature and longer exposure than steam.

💨 Gas

Used in hospitals. Takes longer than steam and requires careful aeration to remove residual gas.

📡 Microwave

Low-pressure steam combined with radiation to produce localised heat that kills microorganisms.

Cycle validation matters: sterilization is only effective when the load, the cycle parameters and the monitoring (biological indicators, chemical indicators, physical parameters) are all correct and documented.

Bringing It Back to the Floor

Your Role — Cleanroom Do's & Don'ts

✔ Always

  • Gown correctly, in the correct sequence, every single time
  • Perform hand hygiene before gloving and after any breach
  • Keep movements slow, deliberate and controlled
  • Stay within your validated area of operation
  • Report any gowning breach, spill or unusual observation immediately
  • Use only approved materials transferred via the approved route
  • Respect contact time when disinfecting

✕ Never

  • Bring cardboard, wood pallets or packing materials into controlled areas
  • Bring cell phones, keys, purses, jewellery or personal items inside
  • Leave skin, hair or street clothing exposed
  • Reach over an open product or sterile surface
  • Rush — rapid movement sheds particles and disrupts unidirectional airflow
  • Assume something is clean because it "looks clean"
Remember: people are the number one source of contamination in a cleanroom — and also the number one control. Your behaviour is a critical control point.
